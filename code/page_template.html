Ino's Japan

In {{facts.first_survey|raw}}, at the age of {{facts.start_age|raw}}, a retired sake brewer called Ino Tadataka set out to measure Japan on foot. Over {{facts.years|raw}} years and about {{facts.walk_km|int}} km his teams paced and chained the coasts and roads, and in {{facts.completed|raw}} the survey became the first map of the whole country drawn from measurement. How close did he get? I put {{source.located|int}} sheets of his large-scale map back on a modern map to find out.

Most old maps in this series are wonderful and wrong. This one is the opposite. Ino Tadataka married into a sake brewing family in Sawara, east of Edo, ran the business for decades, and only after retiring went to Edo to study astronomy. From {{facts.first_survey|raw}} he led a series of surveys that walked the coast of Japan with measuring chains, compasses and a quadrant for the stars, fixing latitudes at night and distances by day. He died before the map was finished; his team completed it in {{facts.completed|raw}}: {{facts.total_sheets|int}} large sheets at 1:{{facts.scale_main|int}}, with the far north at 1:{{facts.scale_hokkaido|int}}, plus medium and small versions.

The Library of Congress holds a manuscript copy of the large sheets, and Wikimedia Commons has photographs of sheets 1 to {{source.last_sheet|int}}. Those {{source.n_sheets|int}} sheets, {{source.pct_of_set|f1}}% of the whole, are what this study uses. They all show the far north: Hokkaido, the southern Kurils and a strip of Sakhalin. I found where each sheet belongs by matching its drawn coastline to the modern one, then measured how far the drawn coast and {{ctrl.n|int}} control points sit from where they really are. Pick a sheet below to see it beside today's map.

02 · How closeWithin a few hundred metres, sheet after sheet

To measure a sheet I kept its scale fixed at the nominal 1:{{facts.scale_hokkaido|int}} and allowed it only one shift and one turn, the best that lays its drawn coast on Natural Earth's modern coast. Then I measured, every kilometre along the drawn coast, how far it lies from the modern line. On a typical sheet the median gap is {{coast.median_of_sheets_m|int}} m; {{coast.within500_median_pct|int}}% of its coast is within 500 m and {{coast.within1000_median_pct|int}}% within a kilometre. On the paper that is about {{expect.coast_mm|f1}} mm. The closest sheet, number {{coast.best_sheet|int}} ({{coast.best_place|raw}}), is a median {{coast.best_m|int}} m out; the furthest, number {{coast.worst_sheet|int}} ({{coast.worst_place|raw}}), {{coast.worst_m|int}} m. {{coast.sheets_under_300|int}} of the {{source.located|int}} sheets are under 300 m.

Half a millimetre on the sheet is {{expect.draw_m|int}} m on the ground. The typical gap is about {{expect.coast_x_draw|int}} times that.

That gap is an upper bound on Ino's error, not a measure of it. Three things add to it besides the survey: the copyists who made the Library of Congress set, the paper's shrinking and folding, and above all the modern line itself. Natural Earth's 1:10m coast is drawn for maps of whole countries; it smooths away small bays and spits, and in places it is several hundred metres from the real shore. A better modern coastline would shrink these numbers, never grow them.

03 · Control pointsCapes, spits and bends

A coastline fixes position across the shore but not along it: a straight beach can slide without the gap changing. For position in both directions I used control points. I picked {{ctrl.hand|int}} capes, spit tips and bends by eye where the sheet and the modern line both show the same feature, and the fit found {{ctrl.auto|int}} more automatically: stretches of {{method.window_km|int}} km of drawn coast that curve enough to be matched to the modern coast without sliding. That is {{ctrl.n|int}} in all, on {{ctrl.sheets|int}} sheets, well short of the {{ctrl.target|int}} I aimed for: Hokkaido's long smooth beaches and the generalised modern coast leave few features that can be matched with confidence.

Measured against each sheet's coastline fit, the control points lie a median {{ctrl.median_m|int}} m from their true positions; {{ctrl.under_1000_pct|int}}% are within a kilometre. Cape Nosappu, the easternmost point of Hokkaido, is {{ctrl.named.Cape Nosappu.res_m|int}} m out; Cape Notoro {{ctrl.named.Cape Notoro.res_m|int}} m; the tip of the Notsuke spit {{ctrl.named.Notsuke spit tip.res_m|int}} m. The largest is Cape Erimo, {{ctrl.named.Cape Erimo.res_m|int}} m, where the sheet draws the point running further out to sea than the modern outline does.

Do the sheets bend as well as shift? On the {{fitq.sim_sheets|int}} sheets with at least four control points, a fit with one shift, one turn and one scale predicts a left-out point to a median {{fitq.sim_loo_median_m|int}} m. Allowing a full affine stretch, on the {{fitq.aff_sheets|int}} sheets with six or more points, does no better ({{fitq.aff_loo_median_m|int}} m against {{fitq.sim_loo_on_aff_sheets_m|int}} m for the simpler fit on the same sheets), and a curved, second-order fit, possible on only {{fitq.quad_sheets|int}} sheets, does worse ({{fitq.quad_loo_m|int}} m against {{fitq.aff_loo_on_quad_sheets_m|int}} m). Within a sheet, the control points show no distortion beyond their own noise.

04 · DistortionSquare to north, true to scale

Each sheet's best turn says how far its north is from true north. On the {{distortion.rot_n|int}} sheets whose coast curves enough to fix the angle, the median tilt is {{distortion.rot_median|f1}} degrees and {{distortion.rot_within2|int}} are within two degrees, from {{distortion.rot_min|f1}} to {{distortion.rot_max|f1}}. The scale holds too. Calibrated on one sheet (Notsuke), the best-fitting scale of the {{distortion.scale_n|int}} sheets with six or more control points ranges from {{distortion.scale_min|f2}} to {{distortion.scale_max|f2}} times the nominal: the sheets agree with each other to within {{distortion.scale_dev_pct|int}}%.

05 · The whole countryLatitude, longitude and the 1878 map

The large sheets have no grid of latitude and longitude, so they cannot say where Ino put Hokkaido as a whole. For that I used a map of the whole of Japan published by the Army General Staff in 1878 and compiled from Ino's survey. I picked {{national.n|int}} capes and island centres on it, from Tsushima to Todoga-saki, the easternmost point of Honshu, and laid it on a modern map with the best shift, turn and scale. The median point is {{national.median_km|f1}} km from its true position, {{national.max_km|f1}} km at most ({{national.max_feature|raw}}).

The 1878 map, with each point's error

Arrows run from the map's position to the true one, drawn ten times longer. Hover a point.

Ino fixed latitudes by the stars and worked longitudes out from his traverses, so the story goes that his north and south were good and his east and west drifted. At this scale I cannot see that. The north-south errors (root mean square {{national.rms_ns_km|f1}} km) and east-west errors ({{national.rms_ew_km|f1}} km) are the same size, about {{national.dlat_abs_median_min|f1}} and {{national.dlon_abs_median_min|f1}} minutes of arc on a typical point, and both are close to what my picking on a {{national.km_per_px|f2}} km-per-pixel image can resolve (three pixels is {{national.pick_km|f1}} km). The one hint is a stretch: if east-west and north-south scales are fitted separately, east-west distances come out {{national.ew_stretch_pct|f1}}% longer (95% interval {{national.ci_lo_pct|f1}} to {{national.ci_hi_pct|f1}}%). Across the length of Japan that would add up to tens of kilometres, but it could equally come from the projection the 1878 compilers chose, which I cannot read at this resolution.

06 · What is here{{source.n_sheets|int}} sheets of {{facts.total_sheets|int}}

This is a small and particular slice of the map. The {{source.n_sheets|int}} sheets are the first in the set's numbering and all show the far north, at the smaller Hokkaido scale. None shows Honshu, Shikoku or Kyushu, where Ino surveyed most densely, so the results say nothing directly about the 1:{{facts.scale_main|int}} sheets of the main islands. Of the {{source.n_sheets|int}}, I placed {{source.located|int}}; {{source.drafts|int}} are unfinished pencil drafts of inland routes, and the rest are almost blank or show too little coast to place with confidence. Ino walked Hokkaido's Pacific coast himself on his first survey in {{facts.first_survey|raw}}; much of the rest of the north on the finished map, including the Kurils and Sakhalin, is usually credited to the surveyor Mamiya Rinzo, so these sheets measure Ino's map rather than only his own legs.

07 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

The sheets. Photographs of sheets 1 to {{source.last_sheet|int}} of the Library of Congress manuscript copy, from Wikimedia Commons, reduced to {{method.width_px|int}} pixels wide. I picked out the drawn coast by colour: dark or red ink lines next to the green hachures that mark land, in strokes long enough not to be labels.

Placing the sheets. A brute-force search tried every position, a range of turns and three scales for each sheet and scored how well its land and sea fell on modern land and sea; I checked the best placements by eye and kept {{source.located|int}}. Each was then fitted to Natural Earth's 1:10m coast by iterative closest point, allowing one shift and one turn with the scale fixed. The scale comes from one sheet (Notsuke, whose hooked spit fixes it) and is carried to the others by the known size of each photograph.

Control points. {{ctrl.hand|int}} capes, spit tips and bends picked by eye on the sheet and on the modern outline, and {{ctrl.auto|int}} stretches of drawn coast, {{method.window_km|int}} km across, matched to the modern coast by a local shift where the coast curves enough to fix the shift in both directions. Their errors are measured against each sheet's coastline fit, which does not use them.

The 1878 map. {{national.n|int}} points picked by eye on a {{method.nat_w|int}} pixel photograph, fitted by the best shift, turn and scale on a conformal projection; errors are split into north-south and east-west parts.

What this is not. A measure of Ino's survey alone: the errors include the copy, the paper, the photographs, my picking and, most of all, the generalised modern coast. A test of the main islands: there are none in this set. A test of absolute position: the large sheets carry no latitude and longitude, so each is fitted on its own.
